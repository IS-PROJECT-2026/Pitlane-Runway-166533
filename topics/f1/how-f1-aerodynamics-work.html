01 / FORMULA 1 AERODYNAMICS

How F1 Aerodynamics Work

Formula 1 cars are designed to move through the air as efficiently and effectively as possible. Aerodynamics determines how air flows around the car and how that airflow can be used to generate grip, control drag and improve performance.

01 / THE BASICS

Why does aerodynamics matter?

When an F1 car moves at high speed, it is constantly interacting with the air around it. The shape of the car changes how that air moves, and the resulting airflow creates forces that affect how the car behaves.

Engineers use aerodynamics to help the car generate grip without creating more resistance than necessary. This is a constant compromise between having enough aerodynamic performance for the corners and keeping drag low enough for competitive straight-line speed.

02 / DOWNFORCE

What is downforce?

Downforce is an aerodynamic force that pushes the car towards the track. It increases the load on the tyres, helping them maintain grip when the car is braking, accelerating or cornering.

More downforce can allow a car to carry more speed through a corner because the tyres can produce more grip. However, generating this force usually involves interacting more strongly with the air, which can also increase drag.

03 / DRAG

What is drag?

Drag is aerodynamic resistance that acts against the car's motion through the air. The greater the resistance, the more effort the car needs to maintain speed.

Reducing drag can improve straight-line performance and acceleration. However, reducing aerodynamic resistance too aggressively can also reduce the amount of downforce available to the car.

04 / THE WINGS

How do the wings work?

The front and rear wings are important aerodynamic devices on an F1 car. They are shaped and positioned to control the way air moves around the car and to help produce aerodynamic forces.

The front wing is particularly important because it is one of the first major components to interact with the incoming airflow. It can influence both the aerodynamic load at the front of the car and the airflow reaching components further behind it.

The rear wing contributes to the aerodynamic balance of the car and can generate significant downforce. It also produces drag, meaning engineers must find a suitable setup for the circuit and driving conditions.

05 / AIRFLOW

What happens to the airflow?

Air does not simply pass over the car and disappear. It moves around, underneath and through different parts of the car. Each aerodynamic surface affects the flow and can influence what happens further downstream.

This means that changing one part of the car can affect another part. Engineers therefore study the complete airflow around the vehicle rather than treating each aerodynamic component as completely independent.

06 / AERODYNAMIC BALANCE

Why does aerodynamic balance matter?

An F1 car needs aerodynamic forces to be distributed in a way that gives the driver predictable control. If too much aerodynamic load is concentrated at one end of the car, the balance can become difficult to manage.

Engineers therefore adjust aerodynamic setup to achieve a balance that suits the circuit. A track with many high-speed corners may require a different aerodynamic approach from a circuit where straight-line speed is more important.

07 / PUTTING IT TOGETHER

Downforce versus drag

Downforce and drag are closely connected. A setup that generates more aerodynamic load can also create more resistance, while reducing drag can reduce the aerodynamic load available to the car.

The goal is therefore not simply to maximise one number. Teams need to find an aerodynamic setup that gives the car the right combination of cornering performance, braking stability and straight-line speed.
